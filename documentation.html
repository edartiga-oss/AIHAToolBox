How the toolbox is organised, how to pick a tool for the decision in front of you, and what to check before you trust an output.

About this toolbox

The Industrial Hygienist Toolbox is a curated directory of exposure assessment tools used by industrial hygiene and occupational health professionals. Each entry summarises what a tool does, which exposure routes and hazards it covers, what inputs it needs, what it produces, and where to get it — so you can choose the right instrument before you start an assessment.

The directory is descriptive, not prescriptive. Tools are listed with their published scope and limitations; deciding whether a given tool is appropriate for a given scenario remains a professional judgement.

How to choose a tool

1. Start from the decision you need to make

Screening a long list of tasks calls for a different instrument than defending a single exposure judgement. Work down the tiers: qualitative or control-banding tools first, refined models only where the decision warrants the extra input burden.

2. Match the exposure route

Inhalation models will not tell you anything useful about dermal loading, and dermal permeation models say nothing about airborne concentration. Use the exposure route filter on the tools page to narrow the list first.

3. Check the applicability domain

Every model was built and validated for a particular set of conditions — physical form, room scale, emission behaviour, substance class. Read the “Known limitations / notes” section on the tool page before assuming a model transfers to your scenario.

4. Confirm you can supply the inputs

Refined models are only as good as their parameters. If room volume, air-change rate, emission rate, or task duration cannot be estimated with reasonable confidence, a screening tool with fewer inputs will usually give a more defensible answer.

Who the tools are for

Worker exposure

Occupational exposure in workplace settings — tasks, shifts, engineering controls, and comparison against occupational exposure limits.

Product assessment

Consumer or downstream-user exposure arising from a product’s formulation, use pattern, and frequency of use.

Environmental assessment

Releases to air, water, and soil, plus indoor fate and transport and community-level exposure.

Assessment categories

Every tool in the directory is filed under one primary category. These describe where a tool sits in an exposure assessment workflow, from first data gathering through to validation.

  • Data Collection (2 tools) — Tools that help structure and organise exposure data gathering. Typically used at the very start of an assessment, before any modelling.
  • Basic Characterization (2 tools) — Simple instruments for initial hazard and exposure characterisation, useful for screening and prioritisation.
  • Preliminary / Initial Exposure Assessment (6 tools) — Qualitative or semi-quantitative tools that produce a first estimate of exposure, often through control banding or a tiered scoring approach.
  • Refined Exposure Assessment (9 tools) — Quantitative models that produce scenario-specific exposure estimates, sometimes including probabilistic or Bayesian treatment of uncertainty.
  • Validating Exposure Assessments (1 tool) — Tools used to test or confirm an exposure assessment against measurement data or an alternative method.
  • Product Exposure Modeling (4 tools) — Models built for consumer product scenarios, accounting for formulation, use pattern, frequency, and consumer behaviour.
  • Environmental Risk / Exposure Assessment (1 tool) — Tools focused on environmental release, indoor and outdoor fate and transport, and community or ecosystem exposure.

Good practice when modelling

Model selection

  • Choose a tier appropriate to the decision — screening versus refined.
  • Confirm the exposure routes modelled match the routes that actually matter.
  • Check that the tool’s applicability domain covers your substance and process.
  • Review validation status and documented limitations before relying on an output.

Data quality

  • Use the best available measured or manufacturer-supplied input data.
  • Document every assumption and its source alongside the result.
  • Run a sensitivity analysis on the parameters the output is most sensitive to.
  • Carry uncertainty through to the conclusion rather than reporting a bare point estimate.

Interpreting output

  • Be explicit about what the number is — task concentration, shift TWA, dose, or risk ratio.
  • Compare like with like when benchmarking against an occupational exposure limit.
  • Consider acute and chronic scenarios separately where the model supports both.
  • Validate predictions against measurement data whenever measurements exist.
